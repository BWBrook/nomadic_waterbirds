# Nomadic waterbirds literature second pass: source-verified review

Prepared 31 May 2026. I unpacked and read the supplied archive (`literature.zip`), which contained the Deep Research review, a triage note, and a source-verification queue. I have treated those documents as an orientation map only, not as verified evidence.

## Scope and verification notes

The emphasis here is on literature and data products that can support a manuscript about hydrological reaction norms of Australia's nomadic waterbirds: dynamic species- and guild-specific responses to current wetness, antecedent wetness, hydroperiod persistence, drying velocity, re-wetting novelty, and regional water availability, with explicit observation-process modelling.

I attempted direct `https://doi.org/<doi>` opening for DOI checks, but the browsing environment refused arbitrary DOI resolver URLs. I therefore marked a DOI as verified only when it appeared in a publisher page, PubMed/PMC, official government data catalogue, institutional metadata page, or other primary metadata source that exposes the DOI or DOI URL. The per-source wording below is deliberately conservative: "DOI verified in publisher/official metadata; doi.org resolver target recorded" means the DOI was not invented and is recorded in source metadata, but I did not observe a live browser redirect from doi.org in this environment. Items without a verified DOI, PMID, ISBN, or comparable stable identifier are placed in the manual-verification section and excluded from the identifier file.

"Use priority" is not a judgement of paper quality alone; it is a practical judgement about whether the source should carry a key manuscript claim, sit behind a methods choice, or remain background.

## Recent state of the art: what has changed since the older foundational literature

The older Australian literature established the central pattern: waterbirds respond to a continent of pulsed, temporary, and often human-modified wetlands, with breeding and abundance shaped by flow magnitude, duration, and landscape context. The 2020–2026 literature changes the manuscript opportunity in three ways. First, telemetry is now rich enough to move beyond general claims of nomadism. The 2024–2025 McGinness/Nicol/Mott cluster shows mixed movement strategies, inland flyway structure, post-dispersal habitat use, connectivity modelling, nesting ecology, and rainfall-triggered long-distance breeding. These papers make a static SDM look conceptually out of date.

Second, the remote-sensing data stack has matured. The Mueller et al. Water Observations paper remains the algorithmic foundation, but the current DEA Water Observations, Water Observations Statistics, Waterbodies v3.0, and the Wetlands Insight Tool provide a stronger basis for operational hydrological metrics: wet/dry observations, clear-observation denominators, hydroperiod frequency, waterbody time series, and wetland-scale vegetation/water/bare-soil dynamics. The newest product metadata also sharpen the limitations: wet surface area is not depth, volume, water source, or habitat quality.

Third, observation-process modelling has become harder to ignore. Hochachka et al. (2023), Johnston et al. (2020, 2023), Fink et al. (2023), and Backstrom et al. (2025) collectively make it difficult to justify naïve use of citizen-science data. The planned manuscript should therefore present its contribution as an observation-aware dynamic hydrology-response model, validated against structured aerial surveys and tested under spatial, temporal, and hydrological-event blocking.

The 2023–2026 papers that most materially affect the planned manuscript are Dunn et al. (2023) on WIT, Hochachka et al. (2023) on eBird occupancy, Backstrom et al. (2025) on citizen-science sampling bias, Brandis et al. (2024) on breeding-site inundation/vegetation, Mokany et al. (2024) on dynamic habitat modelling, Nicol et al. (2024) and McGinness et al. (2024) on tracking/connectivity/habitat use, Becker et al. (2024) on pulse-event trait responses, and Mott et al. (2025) on continental avocet dispersal and rainfall-triggered breeding.

## Do not overclaim

**Hydrological reaction norm.** This is a defensible and potentially elegant framing only if defined as an estimated response surface: the relationship between hydrological state/history and occurrence, count intensity, or breeding probability for a species or guild. It is not, by itself, a genetic or individual-level reaction norm. Avoid wording such as "we estimate plasticity" unless individual histories and appropriate random-slope structures justify it.

**False refugia and ecological traps.** A concentration of birds in a drying wetland is not automatically a false refuge or trap. A trap requires evidence of attraction or preference plus lower survival, reproduction, or recruitment than alternatives. Use "apparent refuge", "temporary concentration site", or "candidate false refuge" unless demographic evidence is present.

**Regional scarcity.** This is a modelled landscape-context metric, not a demonstrated perceptual state of the birds. Phrase as "regional availability of surface water" or "landscape wetness context" unless tracking or behavioural data support stronger movement-cue claims.

**Nomadic response.** Citizen-science occurrence changes are consistent with redistribution or resource tracking, but they can also arise from observer redistribution, detectability shifts, access changes, reporting effort, or true abundance changes. Use tracking and EAWS validation to strengthen movement interpretations.

**Breeding thresholds.** Flow or inundation thresholds are system-, species-, season-, and substrate-dependent. Do not imply universal thresholds across all colonial breeders. Hydroperiod stability, nesting vegetation, predator access, weather, botulism/disease risk, and drying timing can all matter.

**Remote-sensed water equals habitat.** DEA water products are superb for surface-water history, but they do not fully measure habitat. Vegetated wetlands, under-canopy inundation, depth, salinity, turbidity, food availability, nesting substrate, and disturbance may be missing or indirect.

**EAWS as validation.** EAWS is the strongest structured comparator, but it is not pixel-level truth. It has transect design, visibility, timing, and count-error structure. Treat it as an independent structured data stream, not an oracle.

**ALA/Birdata/eBird equivalence.** These are not interchangeable. Complete eBird checklists with effort and protocol information are much closer to detection/non-detection data than incidental ALA records. ALA downloads need project-specific DOIs. Birdata protocol metadata need manual checking.
