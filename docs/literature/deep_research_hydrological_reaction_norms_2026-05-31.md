# Hydrological reaction norms of Australia’s nomadic waterbirds

## Conceptual foundations

### Nomadism, pulsed resources and the reaction norm idea

The most useful starting point for this manuscript is to treat Australian waterbirds as consumers of pulsed, spatially shifting resources rather than occupants of fixed habitat patches. Seminal Australian work showed that the apparent paradox of large desert and inland waterbird populations is best explained by continental-scale access to temporary wetlands whose availability is highly variable through time, while long-term satellite and telemetry studies of Grey Teal and more recent tracking of ibis and spoonbills show that individuals can move hundreds to thousands of kilometres to exploit changing conditions, often with strong individual heterogeneity in movement strategy. More general movement-ecology reviews now frame this kind of behaviour as resource tracking, with nomadism emerging where resources are temporally autocorrelated but spatially unpredictable. [^1]

The phrase hydrological reaction norm is not standard in the waterbird literature, but it is defensible and potentially powerful if used carefully. In behavioural ecology, reaction norms describe how a response changes across an environmental gradient; in this project the term works best as an analogy for species- or guild-specific response curves linking current wetness, antecedent wetness, hydroperiod persistence, drying, re-wetting and regional water availability to occurrence, abundance, breeding or recruitment. Used this way, the concept foregrounds plastic, often nonlinear responses to fluctuating hydrology, rather than implying a narrow quantitative-genetic interpretation. [^2]

Australia is an unusually good model system for this framing because inland freshwater environments are exceptionally variable, river-floodplain systems are strongly boom-and-bust, and many waterbird species show opportunistic or mixed movement strategies rather than fixed migration schedules. Kingsford and Norman’s widely cited review characterised Australian freshwater waterbirds as products of the continent’s ecology, and more recent work has reinforced that point by showing a continuum from residency to nomadism and partial migration within and among species. [^3]

### Distinguishing occurrence, abundance, breeding and recruitment

A major design lesson from the literature is that “waterbird response” is not a single response. Occurrence, local abundance, breeding initiation, brood production, post-breeding dispersal and drought-refuge use are related but ecologically distinct layers. Colonial breeding is especially different from simple wetland use: breeding depends not only on water being present, but on threshold flow events, persistence of inundation for long enough to fledge young, suitable nesting vegetation, adequate surrounding foraging habitat, and often predator buffering by water beneath colony trees or shrubs. Several Australian studies treat these as threshold or persistence problems rather than simple area-of-water problems. [^4]

Recent tracking work sharpens this distinction further. After breeding, royal spoonbills were associated more strongly with reservoirs, marshes and permanent wetlands, whereas straw-necked ibis used a much broader habitat portfolio including terrestrial and agricultural landscapes; similarly, telemetry of ibis and spoonbills showed movement ranging from tens of kilometres over months to hundreds of kilometres over days or weeks. The implication for the proposed manuscript is that a single static wetland suitability surface will miss both life-stage differences and taxonomic heterogeneity in how birds interpret wetness. [^5]

## Hydrology as a pulsed resource template

### Flood pulses, hydroperiod and drying dynamics

The flood-pulse concept remains the most relevant macro-ecological template for this project: in river-floodplain systems, ecological production and biodiversity responses are structured not just by the presence of water, but by the timing, magnitude, duration and recurrence of inundation and by connectivity between channels, floodplains and wetland basins. Australian dryland river ecology made the same point in a regional idiom through the “boom and bust” framing. In waterbirds, this translates into frequent threshold and nonlinear responses rather than simple linear increases with wetland area. [^6]

The Australian evidence base strongly supports inundation, hydroperiod and antecedent conditions as major predictors of abundance and breeding. The Murray–Darling Basin synthesis explicitly modelled lagged wetland area, lagged rainfall and lagged flow because wetland area and rainfall were expected to influence breeding and subsequent recruitment, and lower Murrumbidgee work similarly showed that climatic and hydrological variables shaped total abundance and functional-group dynamics. Colonial breeding studies found threshold responses for all ten focal species examined, with zero breeding probability below thresholds for most species and sharp transitions between non-breeding and breeding for several taxa. [^7]

This literature implies that the manuscript should distinguish at least five hydrological axes. Current wetness matters for immediate habitat accessibility. Lagged wetness matters for productivity, food-web build-up and recruitment. Hydroperiod persistence matters for breeding completion. Drying velocity matters because rapidly falling water can strand nests or collapse foraging opportunities. Re-wetting after drought likely matters because novel or newly inundated areas can attract exploratory or directed long-distance movements. The first three axes are directly evidenced in Australian studies; the latter two are strongly suggested by movement and breeding literature but are far less often parameterised explicitly, which is itself an important gap. [^8]

### Refugia, breeding sites, stepping stones and degraded refuges

One of the strongest Australian results is that wetland roles change with regional hydrological context. Bino, Kingsford and Porter showed that wetlands prioritised for waterbirds in the Murray–Darling Basin vary between boom and bust phases: in dry years, important sites were predominantly riverine and lacustrine refugia, while in wet years priority shifted towards lacustrine and palustrine wetlands associated with more dispersed use and breeding. This directly supports a reaction-norm perspective in which the same site changes ecological role as regional water scarcity changes. [^9]

The same study is valuable because it shows that “refuge”, “breeding-support wetland” and “variable-use wetland” should be treated as distinct ecological functions rather than fixed site labels. In dry years birds concentrate into fewer wetlands; in wet years birds spread across a broader spatial mosaic. A related state-space analysis found broad synchrony in 50 Australian waterbird species across highly variable dryland rivers, again pointing to the importance of landscape context rather than local wetness alone. This means that local covariates should probably be interacted with regional wetness or scarcity metrics in the proposed manuscript. [^10]

The literature is less developed on “false refugia” as a named category, but the underlying idea is still well supported. Studies on drought refuges and ecological traps show that wetlands retaining visible water can cease to function as true refuges when salinity, food resources, vegetation or hydroperiod become unsuitable. In the Coorong, for example, technical modelling and scenario work indicate that poor conditions can diminish drought-refuge function, while broader wetland-change literature warns that degraded wetland function can emerge before complete habitat disappearance. For this manuscript, “false refugia” is best presented as an inferential typology grounded in these literatures, not as an established Australian formal class. [^11]

## Australian evidence base

### Long-term surveys, major trends and hydrological attribution

The long-running aerial surveys are the backbone of inference for Australian inland waterbirds. The national data paper describes three major aerial-survey programs, with the Eastern Australian Waterbird Survey counting waterbirds, nests, broods and wetland area annually since 1983 across up to 2,000 wetlands along ten 30-km-wide survey bands spanning about one third of the continent. These data are extraordinary not only for their duration but because they capture abundance, breeding and wetland extent together. [^12]

The most influential large-scale attribution study remains Kingsford, Bino and Porter’s comparison of the regulated Murray–Darling Basin with the far less developed Lake Eyre Basin. Across 32 years, waterbird abundances were strongly related to river flows and rainfall. The regulated Murray–Darling Basin showed long-term flow declines and consistent declines in waterbird communities at basin, river and wetland scales, whereas the undeveloped Lake Eyre Basin showed no comparable evidence of long-term decline. In the Murray–Darling Basin, 56% of the 50 species analysed declined at basin scale, and projections suggested that environmental-flow recovery could increase numbers but that climate change would erode much of that gain. This remains some of the clearest observational evidence tying large-scale water development to waterbird decline in Australia, while still leaving room for climate change as an interacting future stressor. [^13]

However, the Caley et al. reanalysis is equally important methodologically. It argues that design-based inference from aerial transects is inadequate for many highly mobile waterbirds because year-to-year aggregation and dispersion produce large variation in the proportion of the superpopulation present on surveyed wetlands. Their state-space Bayesian approach incorporated process noise, observation uncertainty and rainfall-derived covariates, confirmed strong positive effects of antecedent rainfall on growth rates for most species, and showed that many species differ in how much of the population is actually available to be counted each year. That is a major warning against simplistic trend estimation and a direct precedent for observation-aware hydrological models. [^14]

### Key systems and exemplary studies

At the site scale, Macquarie Marshes and Narran Lakes remain the classic breeding-flow case studies. Kingsford and Johnson showed that upstream diversions on the Macquarie system significantly reduced colonial breeding, and later work on environmental-flow options found that delivering larger threshold flows less often could increase variability and breeding frequency compared with a low, steady allocation. Macquarie therefore illustrates both the importance of threshold flood events and the inadequacy of treating all wetness as equivalent. [^15]

The Murray–Darling colonial breeding literature has progressively moved from diagnosis to threshold estimation. Arthur et al. estimated breeding thresholds for colonial waterbirds across the basin, showing that below threshold there was effectively no breeding for most species. Bino et al. then used those thresholds to ask how environmental-flow strategies could maximise breeding events. More recently, Brandis and colleagues linked breeding wetlands to vegetation and inundation characteristics, finding that successful breeding wetlands combined specific inundated shrubland extents with dynamic vegetation signals consistent with boom-and-bust conditions. Together these studies make a strong case that breeding reaction norms need threshold, lag and persistence terms, and should not be reduced to the area of open water on the survey date. [^16]

Narran Lakes provides an especially clear example of long-term degradation of breeding opportunity. Brandis et al. used five decades of ibis breeding data to show that upstream diversions reduced breeding frequency, and that even restoration scenarios would leave breeding opportunities well below pre-development levels. The paper is important because it treats breeding frequency itself as the ecological variable of interest, rather than simple occupancy or abundance. [^17]

Other systems matter as contrasts. The Gwydir literature links semi-regular flooding to the maintenance of wetland vegetation structure and, in recent environmental-water reports, to active maintenance of water levels under breeding colonies long enough for young to fledge. The Lower Murrumbidgee/Lowbidgee literature demonstrates strong abundance responses to climate and hydrology. The Coorong and Lower Lakes literature highlights that coastal and terminal systems can act as drought refuges, but that refuge value is contingent on salinity, inflows and food-web condition. Currawinya and Lake Eyre Basin systems illustrate the continuing importance of less regulated inland wetlands as refuges and breeding sites during dry phases elsewhere. [^18]

Recent work also widens the eastern Australian case beyond basin-plan wetlands. A new study of Australia’s largest east-coast wetland complex used up to four decades of assemblage records across 57 sites to identify environmental determinants of spatial and temporal distribution, signalling that the reaction-norm framing can extend to major coastal-floodplain complexes as well as inland dryland systems. [^19]

## Data streams for hydrological reaction norms

### Remote sensing and hydrological covariates

Satellite-derived surface-water products now make it practical to operationalise hydrological reaction norms at continental or basin scales. Water Observations from Space mapped surface water across Australia from every valid Landsat observation from 1987 onward and was explicitly designed to identify the difference between persistent waters such as reservoirs and ephemeral waters such as floodplain inundation. DEA Water Observations classifies pixels as wet, dry or invalid, making it suitable for deriving time series of wetness proportions and observation counts. [^20]

DEA Waterbodies is particularly attractive for reproducible ecological workflow because it supplies ready-made time series for more than 300,000 mapped waterbodies across Australia. But its design choices matter ecologically: only waterbodies larger than 2,700 m² and present more than 10% of the time between 1987 and 2020 are included, and the product deliberately excludes locations seen wet only during extreme flood events. It estimates wet surface area, not depth, volume, water source or purpose, and small or narrow waterbodies are harder to detect. For a manuscript focused on nomadic exploitation of pulsed floodplain resources, this means Waterbodies is excellent for tracking persistent and semi-persistent open water, but insufficient on its own for truly event-driven floodplain habitat. [^21]

The Wetlands Insight Tool partly addresses that limitation by combining open water and vegetation-related cover fractions to visualise water, green vegetation, dry vegetation and bare soil through time within wetland boundaries. Its developers emphasise that open-water products alone miss much of the behaviour of vegetated palustrine systems and seasonal wetlands when they are not inundated. At the same time, WIT still inherits important limitations from optical Landsat data: 30-m pixels, visual obstruction, imperfect performance in densely vegetated wetlands, and cloud-related underestimation of wet periods during rainfall events. [^22]

For this project, the most fruitful hydrological covariates are likely to be derived rather than taken directly from a single product. Evidence-backed covariates include current wet area, annual or seasonal wetness, antecedent wetness over several windows, hydroperiod persistence, and wetland-area lags of about one year, all of which have clear precedents in the Murray–Darling literature. A further inferential step, which appears underdeveloped and publishable, would be to derive explicit novelty metrics such as the historical quantile of current wetness, time since last comparable inundation, or deviation from a wetland’s long-term hydroperiod regime, alongside regional scarcity metrics such as the total wet area in a moving basin window. Those variables are not yet standard in Australian waterbird papers, but the combination of tracking studies, boom–bust wetland theory and DEA time series makes them logically well motivated. [^23]

A final caution is spatial semantics. Official wetland boundaries, Ramsar polygons and DEA waterbody polygons are administrative or remote-sensing objects, not necessarily ecological wetland units. Polycentric floodplains, regulated distributary systems and broad marsh complexes often contain multiple sub-units with different hydrological connectivity and breeding relevance, while open-water products do not resolve within-wetland depth gradients. The manuscript will therefore be strongest if it works from ecologically defined analysis units where possible, or at least tests sensitivity to alternative polygon definitions. This is partly evidence and partly inference from the design of current products. [^24]

### Biodiversity observations and the observation process

The citizen-science opportunity is real, but the observation process must be modelled rather than wished away. eBird’s strengths are well documented: complete checklists can be interpreted as detection–non-detection data, and checklist-level effort variables such as duration, distance, time and observer behaviour can be joined to observations and used in modelling. Recent methodological work on eBird occupancy fitting also stresses that occupancy inference requires repeated visits or carefully defined pseudo-replicates and cannot be treated as simple presence-only mapping. [^25]

Birdata is increasingly important in the Australian context because it combines long-running national bird monitoring with standardised survey options that are directly useful for model calibration. BirdLife Australia describes Birdata as Australia’s longest-running digital bird database with more than 30 million records, and recommends repeatable 2 ha 20 min or 500 m area-search protocols for long-term monitoring. Since late 2024, BirdLife Australia has also formalised collaboration with eBird Australia, allowing BirdLife-standard surveys submitted in the eBird app to contribute to Birdata. That potentially improves interoperability, but it does not remove the need to distinguish structured surveys from opportunistic birding checklists analytically. [^26]

The Atlas of Living Australia occupies a different niche. It is an open-access national aggregator of occurrence records from many providers, and a large share of those records comes from citizen scientists. That breadth is valuable for range documentation and historical occurrence, but the heterogeneity of source protocols means ALA is usually better treated as an occurrence resource than as a direct source of calibrated non-detection inference, unless records can be traced back to checklist-based or standardised protocols. [^27]

Bias is not a minor nuisance in this field; it is one of the central modelling problems. Australian analyses of citizen-science birds emphasise large variation in records among species and uneven coverage across space, time and taxonomy. More general bird-citizen-science analyses show that site-selection bias can be strong, especially where birders preferentially visit attractive or rare-bird sites, and one recent study found that large-bodied birds, common species and birds occurring in large groups are overrepresented in unstructured citizen-science data. For conspicuous wetland species such as pelicans, ibis and large ducks, that is a direct warning. Road bias, access bias, popularity bias and flock-size bias should all be assumed unless tested. [^28]

The most credible pathway is therefore integration rather than substitution. Structured aerial surveys provide standardised, landscape-scale counts of abundance, breeding and wetland area but are temporally coarse and spatially sparse. Citizen science provides fine-grained temporal and spatial coverage but with heterogenous effort and detectability. Integration studies in waterfowl and broader bird monitoring show that combining structured and unstructured data can improve precision and fill monitoring gaps, but can also change trend magnitudes or introduce habitat-related bias if datasets sample different parts of environmental space. Independent validation remains essential. [^29]

## Modelling and validation

### Model families that fit the ecological question

The literature points to a modelling strategy built around multiple linked response layers rather than a single omnibus model. Occurrence on complete checklists can be modelled with dynamic occupancy or checklist encounter models that include observation submodels. Relative abundance from counts can be handled with negative-binomial, hurdle or zero-inflated hierarchical models. Breeding evidence should be analysed separately, either as a binary/ordinal breeding response or as colony-level abundance where breeding records are available. Aerial-survey abundance and citizen-science occurrence can then be joined through shared latent environmental effects rather than collapsed into a single response. This architecture is consistent with eBird methodology, integrated waterfowl modelling, multispecies occupancy work and the Australian state-space treatment of mobile aerial-survey counts. [^30]

For hydrological covariates, the strongest design is likely to be explicitly nonlinear and lagged. The proposed manuscript should estimate species- or guild-specific smooths for current wetness, antecedent wetness, hydroperiod persistence and regional scarcity, with shrinkage or partial pooling across taxa so that sparse species borrow strength without being forced into common response shapes. This recommendation is an inference from several strands of literature: sharp breeding thresholds in colonial species, broad synchrony under regional boom–bust dynamics, strong antecedent-rainfall effects in population models, and dynamic habitat models showing the feasibility of basin-wide fine-resolution predictions for a small set of water-dependent species. [^31]

I would strongly favour a hierarchical Bayesian framework, not because it is fashionable, but because the scientific problem is fundamentally hierarchical. Species differ, functional groups differ, wetlands differ, years differ, observation processes differ, and uncertainty in hydrological covariates and counts can be material. Modern implementations in Stan, TMB or INLA would be well suited. The important point is not the software choice; it is that the model should allow species-specific reaction curves, hierarchical shrinkage, event-year random effects, and explicit observation submodels. That is a methodological recommendation rather than a direct literature claim, but it is well aligned with current best practice. [^32]

A particularly promising structure would be a two-stage model. Stage one would estimate site-date hydrological descriptors from DEA and related products, including derivatives such as persistence and novelty. Stage two would estimate species or guild responses while accounting for observation effort. This avoids trying to infer ecological response directly from raw satellite classifications, and it makes sensitivity analysis on hydrological definitions much easier. If the paper includes breeding, a third linked layer for breeding probability or colony occurrence would materially strengthen novelty. Existing Australian studies have pieces of this puzzle, but not, from the literature reviewed here, the full integrated framework. [^33]

### Validation, blocking and bias tests

Validation should match the way the model will be used, not just maximise apparent accuracy. General SDM literature now shows very clearly that random cross-validation is over-optimistic when spatial or temporal autocorrelation is strong. Ecological best practice is therefore to use spatial, temporal or environmental blocking when predicting transfer across space, time or conditions. For this project that advice should be extended one step further to hydrological-event blocking: entire drought, flood or post-drought phases should be held out together when testing transferability across contrasting water regimes. That extension is an inference, but it flows directly from the structure of the ecological question. [^34]

Independent validation against aerial-survey products is one of the clearest publication strengths available to this project. Citizen-science-derived encounter or relative-abundance surfaces can be compared with Eastern Australian Waterbird Survey abundance, richness, breeding and wetland-area data at matched dates and regions. This would not make the aerial data “truth” in a philosophical sense, but it would provide a powerful out-of-sample benchmark from a fundamentally different observation process. Given Caley et al.’s warning that aerial counts themselves need process-aware treatment, the cleanest validation would probably compare latent hydrological response patterns, rank order of important wetlands, or event-year contrasts rather than raw counts alone. [^35]

Synthetic bias tests would also materially improve the manuscript. These could include downsampling checklists toward roads and towns, inflating records for charismatic flocking species, restricting to hotspot wetlands, excluding incomplete checklists, or weighting observations by local checklist intensity. Comparable studies have shown that accounting for spatial bias can alter occupancy surfaces, and that citizen-science biases are often strongest in where and when observers choose to sample. A simulation appendix focused on whether inferred reaction norms survive plausible observer distortions would be unusually compelling for reviewers. [^36]

## Knowledge gaps and novelty

### What has already been done well

Australian research is already strong in at least five areas. First, the continent has a globally unusual long-term aerial evidence base linking waterbird abundance, breeding and wetland area across multiple decades. Second, the Murray–Darling literature has clearly established relationships among flow alteration, floodplain inundation and waterbird declines. Third, colonial breeding has been analysed with threshold-oriented approaches that are ecologically sophisticated and management relevant. Fourth, movement ecology has advanced rapidly through telemetry, isotopes and habitat-use modelling for a subset of highly mobile taxa. Fifth, the remote-sensing toolbox for wetland hydrology is now mature enough to support reproducible, national-scale analyses. [^37]

### What remains underdeveloped

What is still underdeveloped is the synthesis across these strands. I found strong studies of trends, breeding thresholds, wetland prioritisation, movement routes, breeding-site characteristics and a new generation of dynamic habitat models for a few water-dependent species. I did not find an obvious published Australian study that simultaneously does all of the following: estimates multispecies hydrological response curves; separates occurrence, abundance and breeding as distinct response layers; links those responses to remote-sensed current and antecedent hydrology; models the observation process of checklist data; and validates citizen-science-derived inference against long-term aerial survey data. That absence should be treated as an informed inference from the literature reviewed here, not an absolute claim that no such analysis exists anywhere. [^38]

Several specific gaps stand out. Explicit response modelling for re-wetting after drought, hydrological novelty and drying velocity is sparse. Regional scarcity terms are conceptually supported by synchrony and boom–bust prioritisation studies but rarely estimated as formal interactions with local wetness. Most movement work still focuses on a limited subset of families. Breeding is often studied at colony sites, whereas broader landscape models tend to stop at occurrence or abundance. And although citizen science is now central to bird ecology, Australian waterbird work remains much more developed for aerial surveys than for observation-aware fusion of checklist data and hydrological time series. [^39]

### Is the proposed paper novel and publishable

Yes, with caveats, the proposed manuscript looks novel and publishable. Its novelty will not come from saying that birds respond to water. That is already well established. The novel contribution would be to quantify reaction norms across multiple taxa and response layers in a way that is explicitly dynamic, observation-aware and hydrologically mechanistic. Relative to static SDMs, the contribution would be time-explicit response curves rather than timeless suitability. Relative to wetland atlases, the contribution would be response estimation rather than inventory. Relative to single-species or single-colony models, the contribution would be generality and comparative inference across taxa and guilds. Relative to trend papers, the contribution would be mechanistic structure in relation to current, lagged and regional hydrology. [^40]

The main publication risks are equally clear. If the final analysis collapses into a generic distribution model with “water area” as one predictor, reviewers will rightly see it as incremental. If observation bias is ignored, citizen-science inference will be vulnerable. If breeding is treated as just another presence record, the conceptual framing will weaken. And if regional hydrology is omitted, the paper may miss the central nomadic insight that the same local wetland means different things depending on what else is wet across the landscape. [^41]

## Manuscript architecture and key literature

### Possible paper framings

A strong framing would be Hydrological reaction norms of nomadic waterbirds across a boom–bust continent. This centres the conceptual contribution and suits a multispecies response-curve paper.

A second framing would be From refuges to breeding pulses: scale-dependent hydrological responses of Australian waterbirds. This emphasises regional scarcity, wetland role switching and the distinction between non-breeding concentration and breeding opportunity.

A third framing would be Observation-aware hydrological models for Australia’s nomadic waterbirds. This would foreground the data-fusion and modelling contribution, especially if checklist data are integrated with aerial surveys.

A fourth framing would be Remote sensing the ecology of nomadic waterbirds: lagged wetness, persistence and novelty across Australian wetlands. This is most suitable if the DEA-derived hydrological metrics are methodologically distinctive and central to the paper’s novelty.

A fifth, somewhat more conservation-facing framing would be Dynamic wetland function in Australia: predicting occurrence, abundance and breeding of nomadic waterbirds under changing hydrology. This would sit well in an applied ecology or conservation journal.

### Suitable journals and conceptual figure

If the manuscript is strongest on ecological mechanism and broad relevance, Global Change Biology, Journal of Applied Ecology, Ecological Applications, Freshwater Biology and Conservation Biology are plausible homes. If the comparative multispecies modelling is the best feature, Diversity and Distributions, Ecography or Methods in Ecology and Evolution could fit. If the remote-sensing contribution is substantial and technically original, Remote Sensing of Environment becomes more realistic. If the paper ends up more basin-management focused, Marine and Freshwater Research, River Research and Applications or Wetlands are safer but less ambitious targets.

The strongest conceptual figure would have four panels. Panel A would show the ecological logic: a regional wetness field, a local wetland hydrograph, and distinct response layers for occurrence, abundance and breeding. Panel B would show candidate hydrological metrics through time: current wetness, antecedent wetness, persistence, drying rate, re-wetting and novelty. Panel C would show stylised species or guild reaction curves, for example dabbling ducks peaking at high but ephemeral wetness, piscivores favouring persistent water, and colonial breeders responding to threshold wetting plus sufficient persistence. Panel D would show the observation system: citizen-science checklists and aerial surveys feeding a hierarchical model with independent validation.

### Search terms, seminal authors, datasets and review anchors

The most efficient follow-up search terms are likely to be combinations of: “nomadic waterbird Australia hydrology”; “boom bust wetlands waterbirds”; “lagged wetness waterbird breeding”; “hydroperiod persistence colonial waterbirds”; “rewetting drought waterbirds”; “wetland refuge ecological trap waterbirds”; “dynamic habitat modelling Murray–Darling waterbirds”; “DEA Water Observations wetland ecology”; “eBird occupancy complete checklist waterbirds”; and “integrated species distribution model aerial survey eBird waterfowl”.

The author set most worth following includes Richard Kingsford, Gilad Bino, Kate Brandis, David Roshier, Li Wen, Julian Reid, Heather McGinness, John Porter, Sam Nicol, David Peel, Karel Mokany and collaborators around DEA and Flow-MER.

The core datasets are the Eastern Australian Waterbird Survey and associated national aerial survey archives; Murray–Darling Basin waterbird survey products; breeding-colony monitoring datasets from CEWH, Flow-MER and state agencies; eBird complete checklists; Birdata standardised surveys; ALA occurrence records as a supplementary occurrence layer; DEA Water Observations; DEA Waterbodies; and the Wetlands Insight Tool. [^42]

The review anchors I would keep open while writing are Kingsford and Norman on Australian waterbird ecology, the flood-pulse literature, Teitelbaum on nomadic movement, Abrahms on resource tracking, the Australian waterbird aerial-survey data paper, and the best-practice citizen-science and cross-validation papers. [^43]

## Implications for the nomadic waterbirds paper

The literature supports five clear design lessons.

- Data: combine complete-checklist citizen science with structured aerial-survey data rather than treating either source as sufficient on its own; model the observation process explicitly and keep breeding as a separate response layer. [^61]
- Hydrology metrics: include current wetness, antecedent wetness, hydroperiod persistence and regional scarcity as core predictors; add drying velocity and wetting novelty as explicit hypotheses because they appear important but remain under-tested. [^62]
- Modelling: use a hierarchical, nonlinear framework with species-specific response curves and guild-level partial pooling; avoid a single static SDM and instead estimate linked models for occurrence, abundance and breeding. [^63]
- Validation: use spatial and temporal blocking, but also block by hydrological events such as drought, flood and post-drought phases; validate citizen-science patterns independently against aerial-survey products. [^64]
- Framing: pitch the study as a dynamic ecology and conservation paper on hydrological reaction norms in a boom–bust continent, not as a generic atlas or habitat-suitability map. The publishable novelty lies in comparative, observation-aware, remote-sensing-linked estimation of how nomadic waterbirds respond to hydrological pulses across life-history stages and regional contexts.
